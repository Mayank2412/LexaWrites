### Chapter 6: Terminal Area Constraints and Output Optimization

The architectural boundary of the orbital hydroponic facility reaches its maximum spatial limit upon the completion of the thirty-fifth cultivation day. At this juncture, the active cultivation area designated as `A_cultivation` achieves its final design parameter of one hundred twenty square meters. This physical footprint represents the absolute spatial ceiling imposed by the structural bulkheads of the orbital module. Every square meter within the designated growth rack perimeter is fully occupied by mature plant biomass. The transition from initial seedling establishment to terminal canopy closure dictates a strict adherence to volumetric management, gas exchange coordination, and radiant energy distribution. Within this closed-loop life support matrix, the operational parameters established at the outset of the cultivation cycle govern the final yield calculations and subsystem interactions.

To understand the mechanics of this terminal state, the baseline requirements of the human complement must be evaluated alongside the biological output of the flora. The total crew personnel count is fixed at six individuals. Each crew member demands a specific daily oxygen requirement, designated as `O2_req`, set at zero point eight four kilograms per person per twenty-four-hour period. Multiplying the daily individual consumption rate by the total crew size yields the cumulative daily oxygen demand for the habitat. This calculation establishes a baseline consumption rate of five point zero four kilograms of oxygen per day for the entire crew. The orbital life support hardware relies on the biological canopy to meet this metabolic requirement without relying on emergency compressed gas reserves.

The vegetative canopy generates oxygen as a direct byproduct of photosynthesis, driven by the active cultivation area and the specific daily oxygen yield per square meter of crop canopy, designated as `Y_plant`. This yield factor is calibrated to zero point zero five kilograms of oxygen per square meter per day under nominal lighting and nutrient delivery conditions. When the canopy coverage expands to its maximum terminal limit of one hundred twenty square meters on cycle day thirty-five, the total daily oxygen production reaches six point zero kilograms. Comparing this output against the total crew demand of five point zero four kilograms reveals a net daily oxygen surplus of zero point nine six kilograms. This positive differential validates the sizing parameters of the agricultural hardware and confirms that the maximum spatial allocation is sufficient to sustain the respiratory needs of the personnel payload.

The physical arrangement of the one hundred twenty square meters of canopy requires precise geometric distribution across multiple tiered racks within the orbital module. Gravity-independent fluid delivery systems maintain constant nutrient flow to the root zones, ensuring that the maximum canopy area does not experience localized drought or nutrient starvation. The hydraulic pumps operate at a fixed flow rate of fifteen liters per minute, distributing water and mineral ions from the central reservoir. The primary reservoir capacity is restricted to two thousand five hundred liters, establishing a strict upper boundary for total system fluid volume at any given moment. As the canopy reaches its terminal dimensions on day thirty-five, the total water consumed by the biomass peaks at nine hundred liters per day. This consumption volume represents a significant fraction of the available reservoir capacity, requiring continuous closed-loop reclamation and condensation recovery from the cabin atmosphere to prevent depletion of the fluid inventory.

The lighting regime imposed on the terminal canopy operates on a strict schedule of sixteen hours of active photoperiod per day. During these sixteen hours, arrays of solid-state photon emitters deliver the exact spectral distribution required to maintain the zero point zero five kilograms per square meter daily yield. The remaining eight hours of the diurnal cycle allow for dark respiration and metabolic redistribution within the plant tissues. The synchronization between the sixteen-hour light cycle and the transpiration rate of the biomass dictates the rate of water vapor release into the module atmosphere. The humidity control systems must process this transpired moisture continuously, condensing the vapor and returning the purified water to the two thousand five hundred liter reservoir to maintain mass balance across the closed-loop system.

The achievement of the terminal canopy state on cycle day thirty-five marks the transition from exponential growth management to steady-state system maintenance. Prior to this date, the system operates at various deficit levels where the oxygen produced by the plants fails to match the total respiration demands of the crew. For instance, on the first day of the cultivation cycle, when the canopy coverage is restricted to twenty square meters, the daily oxygen produced is limited to one point zero kilograms, resulting in a net oxygen deficit of four point zero four kilograms that must be offset by stored reserves or secondary life support subsystems. This deficit decreases progressively as the canopy expands through the intervening checkpoint intervals, passing through intermediate coverage milestones at day seven, day fourteen, day twenty-one, and day twenty-eight, until the surplus threshold is finally breached on day thirty-five.

Managing the terminal area constraints requires strict adherence to pruning and biomass harvesting schedules. If the canopy exceeds the allocated one hundred twenty square meters, structural interference with the environmental control and life support system ductwork occurs, potentially blocking atmospheric recirculation pathways and creating localized carbon dioxide pockets. Conversely, any reduction in the active cultivation area below this terminal parameter causes an immediate drop in total oxygen production, dropping output below the five point zero four kilogram threshold required by the six crew members. Therefore, the automation routines embedded within the habitat control software actively monitor canopy density and photon absorption rates to prevent overgrowth while ensuring that every available square meter contributes to the photosynthetic output.

The integration of the botanical subsystem with the wider orbital architecture demands continuous auditing of mass flows. Water entering the system via the two thousand five hundred liter reservoir is routed through the fifteen liters per minute pump network, absorbed by the root substrate, transported through the vascular structures of the plants, and ultimately transpired into the cabin air. The nine hundred liters of water consumed daily at peak canopy capacity must be fully accounted for by atmospheric condensation collectors and direct root-zone drainage capture. Any imbalance in this water loop leads to either flooding of the cultivation chambers or desiccation of the plant tissues, both of which terminate the biological cycle and threaten habitat survival.

The output optimization metrics demonstrate that a cultivation area of one hundred twenty square meters represents the minimum spatial footprint necessary to achieve complete life support closure for six personnel under the defined operational parameters. Altering any single constant—whether reducing the crew count, adjusting the individual oxygen requirement, or modifying the plant yield factor—would destabilize the mathematical equilibrium of the habitat. The deterministic nature of these calculations ensures that plant growth schedules align precisely with the metabolic consumption rates of the human operators. Consequently, the terminal phase of the crop cycle serves as the steady-state baseline for long-duration orbital missions, proving that closed-loop agricultural systems can sustain human life through balanced biomass cycling and controlled gas exchange.
