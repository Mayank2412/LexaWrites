### Chapter 5: Continental Shelves, Trawlers, and Trenching Plows
The moment a transoceanic cable leaves the flat, geologically predictable abyssal plain and climbs the continental shelf, its operational reality changes from an exercise in pressure management to a combat zone against human maritime activity. In deep water, the infrastructure relies on a lightweight profile measuring 17 mm in diameter, which is sufficient because the only threats are tectonic shifts and localized turbidity currents. Once the water depth decreases below 200 meters, however, the primary hazards shift from natural geology to heavy steel and rotational friction generated by commercial vessels. 

To survive this shallow-water transition zone, the cable architecture must change dramatically. The slender 17 mm deep-ocean profile is stripped of its vulnerability and replaced with a ruggedized construction. This shallow-armored variant expands to 50 mm in total diameter by encasing the internal copper and fiber core in double layers of heavy galvanized steel wires. This added mass and tensile toughness prevent immediate shear failures, but physical armoring alone cannot withstand the forces exerted by modern commercial machinery operating on the continental shelf. 

#### The Shallow-Water Threat Matrix
The primary failure modes of subsea infrastructure in shallow coastal zones are overwhelmingly anthropogenic. Statistical tracking of cable faults isolates two dominant mechanical actors in waters shallower than 200 meters:

* Commercial Bottom Trawling: Responsible for 40% of all recorded faults, heavy steel-toothed fishing gear and weighted nets snag exposed cables and exert hundreds of kilonewtons of lateral pull.
* Ship Anchors: Responsible for 30% of shallow faults in depths up to 100 meters, where dragging stockless anchors or high-holding-power anchors hook, stretch, and sever unburied lines.
* Subsea Earthquakes and Turbidity Currents: Account for 15% of faults primarily in steep shelf-canyon transitions between 2,000 and 6,000 meters, where sliding sediment snaps lines instantly.

> Armor protects a cable from friction, but burial protects it from intent. If you leave a telecommunications line resting on the surface of a continental shelf where fishing fleets operate, its lifespan is measured in months, not decades.

#### Mechanical Burial and Trenching Plows
Because steel armoring cannot indefinitely absorb the direct impact of a multi-ton ship anchor or the scraping shear of a bottom-trawling door, engineers rely on mechanical burial. Specialized burial vessels tow massive seabed trenching plows weighing up to 15 tons along the designated cable route. These plows use hydraulic or mechanical blades to slice a trench into the seabed sediments, forcing the 50 mm armored cable down to a target depth of 2 meters. 

In soft silt or sand, jetting ROVs supplement plowing by fluidizing the seabed beneath the cable, allowing it to settle below the active scour zone of trawl gear. Despite these mitigation measures, the continental shelf remains an uncooperative environment. Cable protection corridors are established by international maritime law, forbidding anchoring and dredging within designated zones. Yet, enforcement relies on compliance and continuous AIS monitoring of vessel tracks, making physical burial the final and most critical line of defense against the relentless mechanical activity of the surface world.
