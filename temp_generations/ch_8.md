### Chapter 8: Operational Failure Thresholds & Terminal Radii

The architecture of Roman military logistics operates under strict physical and mathematical boundaries. When the active personnel count within the legion footprint (`N_legion`) is fixed at 5000 individuals, and the daily grain consumption requirement per legionnaire (`C_daily`) is established at 1.5 kg, the gross daily caloric expenditure translates directly into a concrete mass requirement of 7500 kg. This daily consumption rate applies pressure to the pack train infrastructure, which maintains a total train capacity (`total_train_capacity_kg`) of 120000 kg. Operating this supply network requires continuous auditing of reserve draws, foraging yields, and terminal radii to prevent catastrophic logistical failure. 

To understand operational failure thresholds, we must examine the depletion velocity of the 120000 kg total train capacity against the seven-day supply schedule metrics. Throughout the operational window, daily net reserve draws deplete the initial capacity at rates ranging from 2300 kg to 5700 kg per 24-hour cycle. Specifically, Day 1 initiates a draw of 4300 kg, Day 2 demands 4600 kg, Day 3 requires 3400 kg, and Day 4 surges to a peak net reserve draw of 5700 kg due to a contracted foraging yield of only 1800 kg. Day 5 permits a localized recovery with a net reserve draw of 2300 kg, which corresponds to a velocity reduction to 15 km/day and an increased foraging yield of 5200 kg. Day 6 registers a net reserve draw of 5000 kg, and Day 7 concludes the primary matrix cycle with a 3700 kg draw. 

The cumulative sum of these net reserve draws over the standard seven-day matrix dictates the depletion vector of the logistical column. Summing the daily net draws yields a total mass deficit of 29000 kg extracted directly from the transport animals over the seven-day period. Dividing the total initial capacity of 120000 kg by the average daily net draw rate reveals the absolute mathematical limit of unsupported operational endurance. With an average net reserve draw of approximately 4142.8 kg per day, the 120000 kg capacity reaches exhaustion within approximately 28.9 days if zero resupply occurs and foraging yields remain bounded by the 12.0 km maximum effective operational foraging radius (`R_forage`).

However, local resource exhaustion occurs long before the absolute mass limit of the pack train is reached. The maximum effective operational foraging radius (`R_forage`) of 12.0 km defines a circular sweep area of approximately 452.39 square kilometers around any given encampment or march axis. Within this geographic boundary, the available agricultural yield is finite. When a legion executes a march of 25.0 km per day (`V_march`), the time window available for foraging parties to scour the 12.0 km radius is severely restricted. Prolonged stationary encampments deplete local grain stores exponentially, forcing the foraging radius to expand beyond 12.0 km. Exceeding this radius introduces secondary failure modes: security detachments must be larger, transit times consume daylight hours, and animal forage for the 1200 items of pack livestock (`mules_per_legion`) becomes scarce.

The interaction between pack mule capacity (`pack_mule_capacity_kg` = 100 kg) and the total train capacity (`total_train_capacity_kg` = 120000 kg across 1200 mules) creates a cascading vulnerability. As grain is consumed and reserves are drawn down, the total mass of the train decreases, but the proportion of consumable cargo versus animal fodder and tactical equipment shifts. If the net reserve draw exceeds 5000 kg for multiple consecutive days—as observed on Day 4 and Day 6—the logistic train approaches critical mass thresholds where the mules consume a high percentage of the remaining payload simply to sustain their own metabolic requirements.

Auditing the terminal radii requires calculating the point at which the foraging yield drops to zero due to terrain degradation, scorched earth tactics by adversaries, or arid corridor constraints. When foraging yields drop to the Day 4 trough of 1800 kg, the net reserve draw spikes to 5700 kg. If this low-yield condition persists for ten consecutive days, the cumulative reserve draw reaches 57000 kg, reducing the remaining train capacity to 63000 kg. At this juncture, the logistical footprint can no longer support a 25.0 km daily marching velocity without shedding core equipment, reducing personnel numbers, or ordering forced velocity throttling. 

Velocity throttling acts as a primary mitigation strategy against terminal failure. As demonstrated on Day 5, reducing the average daily marching velocity from 25.0 km/day to 15.0 km/day decreases the physical exertion load on the personnel and extends the operational duration within a specific agricultural zone. This velocity reduction permits foraging units to scour a denser portion of the 12.0 km radius, elevating the forage yield from the Day 4 low of 1800 kg up to 5200 kg. Consequently, the net reserve draw contracts to 2300 kg, preserving the structural integrity of the 120000 kg pack train.

Conversely, maintaining a sustained 25.0 km/day marching velocity through low-yield corridors accelerates the approach to the operational failure threshold. On days with high marching distances and depressed foraging yields (such as Day 2 and Day 6), the system absorbs high net draws of 4600 kg and 5000 kg. If such high-draw days occur sequentially without an intervening velocity throttle or high-yield recovery window (greater than 4500 kg forage yield), the pack train depletes its safety margin within fourteen days. 

Terminal radii calculations must also account for animal mortality and payload reallocation. Each of the 1200 pack mules carries a maximum individual load of 100 kg. When total train capacity is audited, any loss of transport animals due to starvation, exhaustion, or terrain trauma results in an immediate step-function reduction in total carrying capacity. The loss of 100 mules removes 10000 kg of capacity from the 120000 kg baseline, forcing the remaining animals to absorb heavier individual packs or requiring the command staff to abandon non-essential hardware, tentage, or reserve rations. 

Operational planners monitor the ratio between net reserve draw and daily forage yield as the primary health indicator of the marching column. When the net reserve draw exceeds seventy percent of the total daily consumption requirement (exceeding 5250 kg out of 7500 kg), the system operates in a deficit state. Sustained deficit states lasting longer than four consecutive operational cycles breach safety margins, forcing commanders to alter the route toward secure supply depots, river transport corridors, or friendly urban centers. The mathematical enforcement of these thresholds dictates that no legion can operate indefinitely without periodic velocity throttling, spatial rotation of foraging parties, or replenishment from fixed logistics hubs.
